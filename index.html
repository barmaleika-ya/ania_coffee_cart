The big idea

Everything a business owns came from somewhere

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

What the business has (assets) was paid for either by borrowing (liabilities) or by the owners (equity, which also includes profit kept in the business). Every transaction keeps this equation balanced, because each one touches at least two places. That is double-entry bookkeeping.

Balance sheet

A photo

What the business has and owes on one specific day. Its official IFRS name is the statement of financial position. “On 30 November the cart has 10,200 zł in cash and owes the bank 5,000 zł.”

Income statement

A movie

Did the business earn more than it used up during the month? Revenue minus expenses equals profit. Profit then moves into equity on the balance sheet.

Statement of cash flows

The bank story

Where real cash came from and went: operating (selling coffee, paying bills), investing (buying the machine) and financing (owner and bank money).

Debit & credit

Dr and Cr in one minute

Every strip shows what the accountant writes, with Dr and Cr in front of each line. They are just the two sides of an account. Debit and credit mean left and right, not money in and money out.

Dr
Debit = the left side
Polish: Wn (Winien) · Russian: Дт (дебет)
Cr
Credit = the right side
Polish: Ma · Russian: Кт (кредит)

The one rule: in every entry, the debits add up to the same amount as the credits. That is double entry, and it is why A = L + E always balances.

That is strip 1, read out loud. Statements never print “Dr” or “Cr”: each account's final balance simply becomes one line, as the Cash T-account in The statements section shows.

    Accounting cycle

    From receipt to report

    Every month an accountant walks the same eight steps. Most of the thinking happens in step 1; the month-end adjustments in step 5 are where profit gets fixed to the right month.

      Ania's trial balance, before and after the adjustments

      A trial balance lists every account with its debit or credit balance. Debits must equal credits. The adjustments column shows the three month-end entries from strips 11–13.

      Watch out: a trial balance can balance and still contain mistakes. If an entry goes to the wrong account, debits and credits still match.

      Easy to mix up

      Common traps for people new to accounting

      These feel obvious but are wrong, and they catch most people who are new to accounting. Each one shows what people often think, what is actually true, and a number from Ania's cart.

      Loans and interest

      ✗“Taking a loan is income”

      Actually: A loan is money you must give back, so it is a liability. Profit does not change.

      Strip 2: cash +5,000 zł and bank loan +5,000 zł. Revenue stays 0.

      See strip 2 ↓
      ✗“All the interest is recorded on the day you borrow”

      Actually: Interest is the price of using money over time. Only the 5,000 zł you received is recorded on day 2. Interest builds up month by month and is recorded as it accrues.

      November's share is 5,000 × 6% ÷ 12 = 25 zł, recorded on 30 November, not 300 zł on day 2.

      See strip 13 ↓
      ✗“A loan repayment is an expense”

      Actually: Each repayment has two parts. The principal part only reduces the debt. Only the interest part is an expense.

      If Ania later pays the bank 1,025 zł: 1,000 zł only reduces the loan. The 25 zł is interest, the only expense part, and it was already recorded as it built up (strip 13), so the payment just clears it.

      Spending money

      ✗“Buying a machine is an expense”

      Actually: Something that will serve for years is an asset. Its cost reaches the income statement slowly, through depreciation.

      Strip 3: 6,000 zł becomes equipment. November's expense is only 100 zł of depreciation.

      See strip 3 ↓
      ✗“Buying coffee beans (inventory) is an expense”

      Actually: Goods on the shelf are inventory, an asset. They become an expense (cost of sales) only when they are sold.

      2,000 zł of beans bought, 1,500 zł used, 500 zł still an asset on 30 November.

      See strip 5 ↓
      ✗“Paying a bill is when the expense happens”

      Actually: The expense happens when something is used up. Paying the bill only settles the debt.

      Strip 10: paying the roaster 1,500 zł changes cash and payables, not profit.

      See strip 10 ↓
      ✗“Rent paid ahead is this month's cost”

      Actually: Only the part used this month is an expense. The rest is prepaid, an asset.

      1,500 zł paid for three months: 500 zł expense in November, 1,000 zł asset.

      See strip 4 ↓
      ✗“The price of an asset is just its sticker price”

      Actually: The cost of an asset includes what it takes to get it working: delivery, installation, set-up.

      If delivering the machine had cost 200 zł, equipment would be 6,200 zł.

      Money coming in

      ✗“Cash from a customer is always revenue”

      Actually: Revenue is what you have earned. Cash for a sale you already recorded is not new revenue.

      Strip 8: the office pays 1,000 zł, but the revenue was counted in strip 7.

      See strip 8 ↓
      ✗“A customer's deposit is revenue” not in Ania's story

      Actually: Money for something you have not delivered yet is owed back in goods or services, so it is a liability (deferred revenue).

      If the office prepaid 600 zł for December coffee, November revenue stays the same; a 600 zł liability appears.

      ✗“VAT on a sale is part of the revenue” not in Ania's story

      Actually: VAT is collected for the tax office and passed on. Revenue is the amount without VAT.

      A 123 zł bill with 23% VAT is 100 zł of revenue and 23 zł owed to the tax office.

      Profit, cash and value

      ✗“Profit means cash in the bank”

      Actually: Profit counts what was earned and used up; cash counts what was paid. They differ almost every month.

      November profit is 2,075 zł, but the bank balance is 10,200 zł, and operating cash flow only 1,200 zł.

      ✗“Depreciation puts money aside for a new machine”

      Actually: Depreciation is only an accounting entry. No cash moves, and no savings pot is created.

      The 100 zł depreciation in strip 12 leaves the bank balance untouched.

      See strip 12 ↓
      ✗“The balance sheet shows what the business is worth”

      Actually: Most assets are shown at cost less depreciation, not at market price.

      The machine shows 5,900 zł even if it could sell for more, or less.

      See strip 3 ↓
      ✗“Equity is money the owner can take out”

      Actually: Equity is a claim, not a pile of cash. It is spread across all the assets.

      Ania's equity is 12,075 zł, but only 10,200 zł of the assets are cash.

      Owner and bookkeeping words

      ✗“The owner's own pay is a wage expense”

      Actually: In a sole-trader business, money the owner takes out is a drawing: it reduces equity, not profit.

      Tomek's 800 zł is an expense; if Ania took 800 zł for herself, it would not be.

      See strip 9 ↓
      ✗“Debit means bad, credit means good”

      Actually: Debit is just the left side, credit the right. Assets and expenses grow with debits; liabilities, equity and revenue grow with credits.

      Dr Cash 10,000 in strip 1 is good news: the cart's cash went up.

      See strip 1 ↓
      ✗““Credit” on my bank statement is the same as a credit in my books”

      Actually: Your bank writes from its own side: your deposit is money it owes you, so it calls it a credit. In your books the same cash is a debit.

      The 10,000 zł arriving shows as a credit on the bank statement and Dr Cash in Ania's books.

      See strip 1 ↓
      ✗“Accumulated depreciation is a liability”

      Actually: It is a minus line under the asset it belongs to, not money owed to anyone.

      Equipment 6,000 less accumulated depreciation 100 = 5,900 zł.

      See strip 12 ↓
